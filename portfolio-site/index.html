Case Study

The 216-Campaign Forensic Audit

Context

A financial advisory company serving a German-market audience, running weekly email campaigns across banking and property verticals, with no systematic framework for content selection.

Problem

Content was inconsistent, angles repeated without anyone tracking it, and there was no way to know which topics actually moved engagement.

Approach

Audited all 216 past campaigns, tagging each by topic, angle, vertical and engagement result. Built a three-tier content framework and a set of content angles from the findings, plus a library of 20 reusable email frameworks with rotation rules.

Result: A content operating system derived from data, not instinct — a structured rotation where every send has a purpose instead of starting from a blank page each week.
Case Study

The Webinar Email Engine

Context

The company runs a weekly webinar for its German-market audience as its primary conversion event, with three distinct populations — never registered, registered but never attended, and past attendees.

Problem

Early campaigns treated the database uniformly. Engagement in the past-attendee segment decayed badly over several months of uniform, repeated sends.

Approach

Designed a three-tier invite system with different cadence and copy treatment per tier, produced weekly, with a locked, stress-tested post-webinar template and mandatory formula rotation so no contact sees the same approach twice in a row.

Result: A webinar invite system that treats segments as distinct populations, with formula rotation built in to prevent fatigue.
Case Study

The Scandinavian Re-Engagement Sequence

Context

Sales was preparing to cold-call 481 Scandinavian leads who had gone quiet. Cold outreach to fully disengaged contacts has a predictable failure rate.

Approach

Designed a three-email sequence over ten days, segmented by engagement tier, each email doing a distinct job: re-establish context, provide value, create a reason to respond before the call.

Result: A reusable re-engagement framework bridging marketing and sales — instead of a cold list of names, sales received contacts who'd recently had relevant contact.

Architecture

Lifecycle State Model

A mutually exclusive state system where every contact sits in exactly one current state, while sticky history flags track everything they've ever done. A resolver computes state automatically from precedence logic.

Qualification

Rebuilt lead scoring from a 40-point to a 20-point scale using 200+ historical campaigns as the evidence base, with a dual-gate qualification rule rather than a single threshold.

Engagement

Philosophy

Sending Emails Is Not Email Marketing

I used to think email marketing was relatively straightforward. Write an email. Send it to a list. Look at the open rate. Send another one next week. The longer I worked with email, the more I realised that was not email marketing. It was just sending emails.

Email is one of the few channels where a person gives you ongoing permission to communicate with them directly — until they decide what you're sending is no longer worth receiving. That distinction changed everything for me.

Permission marketing is the privilege — not the right — of delivering anticipated, personal and relevant messages to people who actually want to get them.Seth Godin

Once you treat someone's inbox as a privilege rather than a channel, the approach changes. You stop asking "what should I send this week?" and start asking "what has this person told me they care about?" Every open, click, ignore, download or registration is information — and once you treat it as information rather than just a metric, email becomes a qualification system, not a broadcast tool.

The Microwave and the Oven

⚡ The Microwave

High-intent leads who've demonstrated enough engagement that generic educational content makes little sense. Shorter path, more direct communication, accelerated toward conversion.

🔥 The Oven

Contacts who need weeks or months of education before they have enough trust or urgency. You don't keep opening the door every five minutes asking why they're not ready — you let the process work.

A person enters. Their behaviour creates information. That information changes what they receive next, changes their score, changes their lifecycle stage, changes the communication — which generates another behaviour. The cycle continues, not linearly, but as a system that responds to the person inside it. That's what I build.
